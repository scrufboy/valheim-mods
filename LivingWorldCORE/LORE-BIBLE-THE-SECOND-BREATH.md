# Living World C.O.R.E. lore bible: The Second Breath

Status: canonical story authority for origins, draugr villages, wilderness
companions, reclamation, and the player's purpose.

## Canon promise

Living World extends Valheim's fiction without claiming its new events occur in
surviving Norse sources. Attested ideas are foundations; the Second Breath, the
Hearthless, and the camp histories are Living World inventions designed to fit
between them.

Valheim asks the player to prove worth to Odin and reclaim a long-forgotten land
from the Forsaken. The *Prose Edda* describes Odin as father of the slain and the
einherjar as chosen battle-dead. *Grímnismál* gives him Huginn and Muninn, yet has
Odin fear that his own gatherers of thought and memory may not return. Odin seeks
knowledge elsewhere; the sources do not require him to know every human heart.
The Norns allot lives, and even gods move inside a doom they do not simply erase.
*Hávamál* makes caution, hospitality, reciprocity, reputation, and freely
maintained friendship central human obligations.

Living World's premise follows:

> Odin knew deeds, deaths, victories, and usefulness. He did not know every heart.

He drew proven and promising dead into Valheim believing hardship would turn
them into willing instruments. Some accepted his war. Some served only long
enough to survive. Some refused to exchange one lord's command for another's.
They wanted hearth, kin, work, and peace. Valheim would not permit peace without
defense, so they learned to fight for one another instead of for Odin's game.

## The Second Breath

Everyone brought from Midgard died there. They are neither ordinary living
travellers nor transparent ghosts. Passage into Valheim gives the dead an
embodied **Second Breath**: hunger, cold, pain, blood, fatigue, sleep, work, love,
and a death that can matter again.

This explains why human companions can eat, wear armor, build, bleed, die, and
persist while sharing the player's afterlife origin. It does not make every dead
person an einherji. That word remains reserved for Odin's chosen slain of Valholl
in the mythic sources. Valheim's people call themselves by homes, bands, trades,
vows, and earned names—not by a universal divine rank.

## Before the player

The opening dreams are memories of a time the player did not witness. Earlier
arrivals raised halls, fenced fields, cut roads, buried friends, traded across
biomes, and fought the Forsaken and one another. Their works explain why the
world feels inhabited before anyone answers the player's call.

Odin judged these settlements by usefulness. Their people judged them by whether
a child could sleep, a guest could be warmed, and the dead could remain at rest.
That difference became the first fracture.

Some communities kept answering divine demands. Some hid. Some broke into war
bands. Some made bargains with powers already rooted in Valheim. Some became the
small camps and guarded roads now encountered by the player.

## Why the villages are full of draugr

The draugr are not NPCs waiting to be switched back into living villagers. Norse
saga tradition presents draugar/aptrgangar as embodied revenants: dead who remain
in or return to corpses, haunt property and mounds, and assault the living. Living
World uses that model.

When the old settlements collapsed, three things held many dead to their bodies:

- **Possession:** refusal to release hall, treasure, authority, or grievance.
- **Broken obligation:** abandoned guests, betrayed kin, violated sanctuary, and
  oaths used to justify cruelty.
- **Foreign corruption:** the Forsaken, swamp rot, hostile seidr, and burial
  grounds saturated by repeated violent death.

Not every draugr was wicked in life. Some were trapped by another person's oath,
raised to defend a hall, or caught in a whole settlement's ruin. The tragedy is
that the revenant remembers possession more clearly than personhood.

Killing a draugr **releases the bound dead from corrupted flesh**. It does not
restore that corpse as a recruit. Reclamation makes the hearth safe; living
Second-Breath survivors then return from camps, refuges, and wandering bands.
Occasionally a named revenant may be freed through a special story, but this is an
exception requiring identity evidence, not the population rule.

## The Hearthless and the Hearthbound

Those who refuse Odin's endless contest are collectively called the
**Hearthless** while they wander and the **Hearthbound** after they establish or
join a defended home. These are conditions, not races or permanent factions.

They are wary of heroes because heroes bring attention, raids, rival claims, and
the commands of gods. They may admire the player and still refuse. They may need
help and still keep a spear between themselves and a stranger. Their central
question is not “Are you powerful?” but “What does your strength serve?”

## Huginn, dreams, and discovery

Huginn does not provide a companion catalogue. He teaches the player that other
Second-Breath people exist and that signs of habitation can be read. Dreams show
past hearth smoke, silhouettes on roads, a hand raised from a distant ridge, and
villages before their fall. These are fragments of thought and memory, not a
complete map.

The player always knows others may be out there. Finding them still requires
travel and attention. Camp smoke, worked stumps, butchered game, footprints,
warding marks, distant shouts, and brief signals lead to encounters.

## The player's purpose

Odin asks the player to prove worth by defeating the Forsaken. Living World adds
a human answer: prove what that worth is for.

The player may free bound dead, make roads and hearths safe enough for people to
return, recognize allies by choices instead of appearance, offer sanctuary
without ownership, defend those who say yes, respect those who say no, and build
a coalition without becoming another tyrant.

The story is not rebellion because Odin is secretly a simple villain. It is the
discovery that even a god gathering heroes for a necessary war could mistake
usefulness for consent.

## Recruitment: the plain question

There is no recruitment minigame, affection meter, dice roll, or concealed
persuasion contest. The encounter establishes the facts. The player asks:

> Will you come with me?

The answer is **yes** or **no**.

The answer is deterministic from what the NPC knows: present danger, prior harm,
faction allegiance, personal goal, obligations to companions, available home,
role fit, and what the player did. Personality changes how the answer is expressed,
not whether hidden rules reroll it. A refusal changes only when world facts change.

Yes is not ownership. The person keeps name, history, equipment, limits, and the
right to defend home. No is not punishment. The player may still trade, help,
share a fire, or meet that person again.

## Truth labels for writers

Every lore entry should distinguish:

- **Attested foundation:** supported by an identified Norse source.
- **Valheim canon:** stated or shown by Iron Gate's game/material.
- **Living World canon:** this project's binding invention.
- **Character belief:** may be incomplete, biased, mistaken, or factional.

Characters should disagree about Odin, draugr, fate, and the Forsaken. The bible
defines what happened; dialogue reveals only what a person believes.

## Research anchors

- [Valheim official FAQ](https://www.valheimgame.com/faq/) — reclaiming Valheim
  and proving worth to Odin.
- *Grímnismál* 20 — Huginn and Muninn range over the world and Odin fears their
  loss; public-domain Bellows translation is in the
  [Poetic Edda source archive](https://hrafn-wikinger.com/en/sources/poetic-edda/grimnismal/).
- *Hávamál* 1–4 and gift/friendship stanzas — caution at doors, warmth, food,
  welcome, reciprocity, and maintained friendship.
- *Völuspá* and *Gylfaginning* — Norns allot lives; divine knowledge exists beside
  fate rather than abolishing it.
- *Gylfaginning* — Odin as Valfather and the battle-dead gathered to Valholl.
- *Grettis saga* and related saga tradition — embodied revenants bound to bodies,
  mounds, property, and violent return.
